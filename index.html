The Lost Middle

Bury it in the middle and the model won't find it

Feed a language model a long context --- a long chat history, a stack of retrieved documents, a big pasted file --- and it doesn't treat every part of it equally. A fact placed at the very start or the very end tends to get noticed. The same fact placed in the middle tends to get lost, and the longer the context, the worse the burial. This is the "lost in the middle" effect, and it matters every time you decide where to put the thing you actually need the model to use.

Try it

Picture a context window as a row of chunks --- paragraphs, messages, documents. One chunk holds the fact you need the model to use. Drag it around, stretch the context, and watch the model's estimated recall move.

Why the middle loses

Models built on attention don't scan a context evenly --- they weigh some positions more than others, and in practice that weight skews toward the start and the end. Liu et al.'s "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (2023) found this U-shaped pattern across several models: accuracy on a fact retrieval task was highest when the relevant document sat at the very start or end of the input, and dropped sharply for documents placed in the middle --- worse still as more documents were added.

A note on the numbers above: the curve you moved is a hand-tuned illustrative model of that U shape, not the paper's own measurements --- exact numbers vary by model, task and prompt, and the paper doesn't publish a single curve you could copy in here honestly. What does reliably hold, across the models it and later work have tested, is the shape: worse in the middle, and worse again as the context grows. If you're deciding where to put the one fact a model absolutely has to use, that shape is the part worth keeping in your head.
