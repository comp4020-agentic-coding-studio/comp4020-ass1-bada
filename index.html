The Lost Middle

Bury it in the middle and the model won't find it

Whether a model uses a fact depends less on whether you included it than on where it landed. The same fact --- same words, same prompt --- is picked up reliably at the very start or the very end of a context, and missed in the middle, where it may as well not have been supplied at all. The longer the context, the wider that dead zone. That's the "lost in the middle" effect: position is doing work you probably assumed content was doing.

Why the middle loses

The instinct when a model doesn't know something is to hand it more: paste in another document, extend the chat history, retrieve a few more search results. "Lost in the middle" is the inconvenient finding that this can backfire --- the extra material mostly serves to bury the one fact you needed in the position the model reads worst. Models built on attention don't scan a context evenly --- they weigh some positions more than others, and in practice that weight skews toward the start and the end. Liu et al.'s "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (2023) found this U-shaped pattern across several models: accuracy on a fact retrieval task was highest when the relevant document sat at the very start of the input, nearly as high at the very end, and dropped sharply for documents placed in the middle --- worse still as more documents were added.

A note on the numbers above: the curve you moved is a hand-tuned illustrative model of that U shape, not the paper's own measurements --- exact numbers vary by model, task and prompt, and the paper doesn't publish a single curve you could copy in here honestly. What does reliably hold, across the models it and later work have tested, is the shape: worse in the middle, a touch worse at the very end than the very start, and worse again as the context grows. If you're deciding where to put the one fact a model absolutely has to use, that shape is the part worth keeping in your head --- and if you only get one edge, make it the start.
